The product holds 80 latent leads — a fixed list the team cannot see, each carrying a potential satisfaction between −1 and +1. The positive ones are real features; the rest, about half, are non-features best never shipped. Research uncovers them, framing sorts them, ideation and prototyping improve them, testing filters them. Five sliders share a fixed budget of 2.50 — raising one station forces another down. A sixth sets the schedule pressure. Delivered satisfaction earns money; every sprint costs some. Run out of capital and you lose; reveal the whole catalogue and you win.

How to read the park

Two outer gates, always open. To the north, users reveal new leads and unblock the ones coming back from a rejection; to the west, they judge what is put in front of them.

The selected station is circled by an animated ring on the map, in the colour of its card. Click a building, or press 1–5.

Lead, feature, non-feature. The catalogue holds 80 leads; the only way to learn which are worth anything is to explore them. A lead with positive potential is a feature, one with negative potential a non-feature. The counters say "leads" until the verdict is in.

The ring under each box shows its delivered satisfaction: potential × real + differentiation. Red, the lead will harm users; grey, it will do nothing; green, it is worth building. You can see that truth — the team has to estimate it.

Every three-week sprint reveals a sample of leads and drops it into the backlog, whether or not it can be absorbed. Leads enter the park as visits happen and space allows, twelve at a time; the rest piles up. Schedule pressure makes stations get skipped: their effect is lost for that lap, and the station roof fades.

A non-feature that reaches production costs four times what a feature earns. That is what the two filters are for, and not funding them is paid in cash.

Two filters, different in kind. At Framing, a lead can be dropped: the box turns grey and vanishes. At Testing, it is merely rejected and goes round again. Killing an idea at Framing costs one segment; killing it at Testing costs a full lap.

At Testing, the lead waits for a user to show up. Kept, it lifts off. Rejected, it goes round again and gains more real satisfaction and differentiation — but a firmly negative potential never recovers: after six laps, it is abandoned.

The budget is fixed: the bar at the top of the panel shows the split, the hatched part what is left unspent. The game opens on the allocation of a team that builds first and talks to nobody — 0.75 of the budget sits idle and nothing catches the non-features. Redoing it is your job: left alone it wins about one game in five, against nearly three in four once the whole budget is spent.

Capital starts at 195. Each sprint takes 8 plus 6 per unit of allocated resource; each release pays 75 times the satisfaction delivered — and a shipped non-feature costs four times what a good one earns, at equal satisfaction. Nothing pays for time passing: one more sprint is one sprint lost.

The trap is the opening — you pay for a dozen sprints before the first release. Rushing research reveals the catalogue fast but funds nothing: it is the most common way to go bankrupt. Click a building to inspect it. Space pauses, 1–5 opens a station card.

The geometry encodes the double diamond: the path widens mid-segment, where the work diverges, and pinches at each station, where it converges. Iteration has no building — it is one more lap of the circuit, which is what it actually is. The product draws 80 potential satisfactions uniformly between −1 and +1: half the latent leads are non-features before any sorting, which is a convenient working assumption, not a measurement. The coefficients are invented to make a causal chain visible; no figure here is a benchmark to quote.
